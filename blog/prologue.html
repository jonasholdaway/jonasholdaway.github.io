July 2026 – 15 September 2026


Did I actually get deported from Germany? Well, not exactly.

...

It was the last week of July, and nearly everything was packed: my luggage for Zimbabwe, my suitcase for France, my boxes for Berlin. I put all my affairs in order. I visited friends and family. I said all my goodbyes. And then I received an email from the Tesla immigration team: "Have you completed your meeting with the consulate?" ...Meeting? They'd never mentioned a meeting. In a panic, I checked the online portal of the German consulate to discover that my Blue Card visa had been PRE-approved, not fully approved. Worse yet, all the meeting time slots were after August 1, the departure date for the multi-continent journey which would eventually bring me to my new home. In retrospect, it's clear that you'd need an in-person meeting to receive a visa. But leaving my trust in the immigration team, I hadn't thought of it. I emailed them back, frantic. They had no solutions. I called the German consulate -- after 2 hours on hold over 2 days, I finally connected with a human. Even if a meeting were open, the consulate explained, they'd need to hold my visa while processing the approval. It couldn't be done.

So I had no other choice: I would travel to Germany, and sort out my visa there. The consulate told me the wait times in Berlin were months long. The immigration team told me they could no longer help. But here's a little secret: never underestimate the power of *believing in yourself*. Mind. Over. Matter. The morning before I flew out of California, my Tesla recruiter called. "We STRONGLY recommend that you return to California to obtain your visa," she told me verbally and in her follow-up email. "Do not pass GO. Do not collect $200." And my facade of unstoppable stoicism crumbled.

Hours later, I left for the journey of a lifetime, with a Blue Card-shaped void gnawing away in the center of my brain. This blog is about Germany, so I won't dive into the travels that preceded it. To overview, I spent two weeks on safari in Zimbabwe with my dad, traveling from Mana Pools to Hwange to Victoria Falls. The wildlife, the landscapes, and the people created a truly majestic experience. I had gone hoping that multiple days of exploration would let us see antelope or zebra, even elephant if we were lucky. In reality, the fauna were so abundant that you couldn't drive from airstrip to cabin without stopping for an elephant family in your path. I then spent a week in France with my mom; Paris might be the most amazing city I've seen, and the powerful history of Normandy proved more impactful than I could have foreseen. From France, we flew to Berlin for one final week, joined by my cousin Tegan. Where Paris had been some form of a real-life Disneyland, Berlin felt like a city of grit and spunk, with more to uncover at every corner. The aftermath of the war still defined the city. At week's end, instead of moving into my new apartment and starting my new job as intended, I took my third intercontinental flight of the month. Through the Airbus window, I gazed out over Berlin. "I'll be back."

The first business day back in California, I drove up to Los Angeles at 5 o'clock in the morning (I still hit traffic). During my travels, I'd booked the earliest possible meeting slot. I arrived at the consulate 80 minutes early, dressed in full slacks, button-up, and tie to show I meant business. When the time came and the officer asked for my papers, I pulled out a manila folder with my name on it. Application form. Passport. Driver's license. University degree. Biometric photo. How'd it go? I crushed it. Time to book a new flight to Berlin.

...

Thanks to my unforeseen troubles, I'd needed to cancel my apartment booking in Friedrichshain, the epicenter of Berlin's youth scene (and its legendary nightlife). I booked a new room in a slightly less appealing Friedrichshain apartment and paid the first month's rent, starting September 10, reasonable time before my new start date, the 16th of September. Side note: while I was freaking out over my deportation situation, my team at work remained entirely patient and understanding. My boss even sent a very thoughtful email, mentioning how she had visa issues when immigrating to Germany as well.

My new flight date inched closer, but one big problem: I didn't have the address for my new apartment. The landlord continually delayed my requests or ghosted my emails entirely. With no actual confirmation of an apartment across the water, I booked 5 days at a hostel the morning of my flight. At the airport, the landlord finally replied that I could move in on the 15th. The day before work started. Nothing I could do about it. I flew from Orange County to San Francisco, then to Frankfurt. As I impatiently bustled through customs to make my final leg to Berlin, I got a new update. The landlord canceled my booking entirely. If at any point I'd thought my panic couldn't get higher, I was wrong.

Arriving near midnight in the capital without fanfare, I immediately crashed in the hostel. I woke up for my first day living in Berlin, which happened to be the day Rosh Hashana began. Fortunately, my Aunt Marci had connected me with the CEO of Hillel Deutschland (!). Super clutch. At Hillel, I met a whole range of 20- and 30-somethings from nations around the world. The next two days of Rosh Hashana, I checked out a couple congregations: a historic synagogue, Fraenkelufer, which amazingly survived Kristallnacht and WWII, as well as a more modern congregation, Bet Haskalah, hosting services in a children's music school. I really clicked with Bet Haskalah. The group was very small, welcoming, and international (that said, I'd hardly met another American so far).

Meanwhile, I reached out to the landlord of another apartment I'd previously considered. I liked that it was walking distance to work in Köpenick, a smaller town outside the city center, but had eventually chosen to live closer to the action. Alas, the luxury of choice was no longer mine. After some back-and-forth, I convinced the landlord to allow a September 15 contract signing with same-day move-in. I re-packed all my belongings from the hostel and, miracle of miracles, moved into my new, 4-person flat. Wohnung, süße Wohnung.

To call my move-in a rocky start would be an understatement of tectonic proportions. My room, which apparently had not been cleaned before I arrived, contained two couch-beds, one IKEA Kallax unit, and one nonfunctional floor-to-ceiling fireplace (??). I walked down the road to Woolworth (apparently they're still in business in Germany) for basic linens. There, I discovered that German bedding operates rather differently than in the US. After several rounds of charades with the unsmiling employees, I left with a complete standard set: fitted sheet, duvet, giant 80 × 80cm pillow, duvet cover, and giant 80 × 80cm pillow cover. Somehow, though the quality was so cheap, the total bill ended up very high.

I returned "home," but cooking was not an option because: a) I had no food, b) I had no fridge or cabinet space, c) the kitchen was a seemingly long forgotten warzone of dishes and half-eaten pasta. Even if I had the energy to clean, the kitchen did not have any SOAP. How??? I am a person who does not need much to get by. But if I have an unclean kitchen in my home, my soul will have no peace.

Well, if I'm not eating, might as well shower and sleep. In the bathroom, I discovered that there was no toilet paper holder, nor any toilet paper. Again: how??? Meanwhile, the shower is some 2000s era futuristic pill-shaped capsule with flashing lights and FM radio and an assortment of buttons and knobs, crusted over in white limescale. I managed to activate the lights and the radio speakers, but no combination of button presses and knob twists would activate the water (arguably the most important aspect of a shower). Instead, I took a wonderfully comforting bucket-style shower in the separate bathtub, like some sort of Victorian peasant.

While I'm making my couch-bed, I catch a figure passing by my doorway. Finally, another human.
"Hi!" I call out. "I'm Jonas."
She nods and replies, nonplussed, "Oh, you live here?"
"Yea—" before I can respond, she's already walked on.
I am baffled. She didn't even introduce herself? She hardly even broke stride when she an unknown man appeared unannounced in her apartment. Well, then. Looks like I won't be finding out how the shower works. Or the WiFi passcode. Or the deal with the kitchen...

Hungry, partially clean, and teetering on the edge of mental breakdown, I collapsed on one side of the crack running down the middle of my couch-bed and laid my head on the pillow whose plastic anti-theft tag Woolworth had failed to remove. T-6 hours until my first day at my first job!
